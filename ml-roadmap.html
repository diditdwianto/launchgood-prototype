Campaign Trust Copilot · engineering note

From decision log to model

What it would take to put a trained risk model behind campaign review — and why the binding constraint is labels, not algorithms.

Written against the current system: a LangGraph pipeline where deterministic checks gather evidence, a language model reads it, a formula scores it, and a human decides. This note covers replacing the formula.

01The constraint is labels

Nothing here is blocked on model architecture or infrastructure. Both are solved problems for this shape of task. The thing you do not have is a few thousand campaigns with known outcomes.

The useful consequence: the review console already in production is the label generator. Every decision a reviewer records — the campaign, the evidence that was available at the time, what the AI recommended, what the human actually did — is one training row. That table is the asset. The model is downstream of it.

Practical read

At zero labeled examples, a model is realistically 12–18 months out. The highest-value work now is instrumenting so the dataset exists when you want it, which is section 08.

02Decide what you are predicting

This is where most fraud-ML projects fail, and it fails early and quietly. Three candidate targets, ordered by how soon the labels arrive.

TargetLabel sourceLagCatch
Reviewer decision
approve / reject / escalate		 Decision log None Predicts your reviewers, not fraud. Inherits every bias they have.
Bad outcome
chargeback, clawback, takedown, complaint		 Payments, support, trust & safety 3–12 mo Rare and delayed — but it is the only real signal.
Needs escalation Decision log None Not a fraud model at all. A triage model, and the cheapest useful one.

Start on reviewer decision as a shadow model, because you can start this quarter. Treat bad outcome as the target you migrate to once enough of them have accrued. Be explicit in writing about which one a given model was trained on — the two get conflated constantly, and they justify very different actions.

Trap: the feedback loop

If you train on reviewer decisions while reviewers are looking at AI recommendations, the model learns to predict its own past output, and accuracy climbs while real-world value does not.

Log whether the reviewer saw a recommendation before deciding. Keep a slice where they do not — an unassisted holdout is the only clean signal you will have, and it is far cheaper to start collecting now than to reconstruct later.

Trap: reject inference

You only observe outcomes for campaigns that were approved. Rejected ones have no counterfactual, so the training data is censored by your own past decisions and the model inherits them as ground truth.

This is the classic credit-scoring problem and has known treatments. The important part is recognising you have it — untreated, it produces a model that confidently reproduces yesterday's mistakes.

03Data requirements

  • Volume. For gradient-boosted trees on tabular features, a few thousand labeled rows minimum, with several hundred positives. At a 1–5% fraud base rate that implies roughly 10k–100k campaigns to get there.
  • Point-in-time correctness. Every feature must hold the value known at submission, not today. "Organiser has 3 prior campaigns" computed at training time is a leak that will make offline metrics look excellent and production performance collapse.
  • An immutable event log. Append-only, timestamped. Mutating a campaign row in place destroys your ability to reconstruct what was true when the decision was made.
  • Outcome plumbing. Chargebacks and clawbacks live in the payments system. If they are never joined back to the campaign, the good target from section 02 stays permanently unavailable.

Already correct in the current build

The assessments table snapshots the evidence bundle as it stood at assessment time rather than recomputing it. That is the point-in-time property, and it is the expensive one to retrofit. Keep it.

Expect the missing three to outperform everything above them. In practice a fraud ring is caught by a shared payout account, not by prose analysis. Content features find the careless; linkage features find the organised.

05Model choice

Gradient-boosted trees — LightGBM, XGBoost or CatBoost — on tabular features.

For tabular fraud this consistently beats deep learning, trains in seconds, runs on a CPU, and supports per-prediction SHAP attributions, which matters because reviewers need a reason and not just a number.

  • Text — sentence embeddings reduced and fed in as features, or a small separate classifier whose score becomes one feature.
  • Images — perceptual hashing for near-duplicates; CLIP embeddings for stock-photo and reverse-image detection.
  • Calibration — isotonic or Platt scaling, applied on a held-out slice. Boosted trees are badly calibrated out of the box and you need a genuine probability to threshold on.

Do not fine-tune a language model for the score

It is more expensive, worse on tabular data, harder to explain, and it reintroduces exactly the reproducibility problem the current architecture was built to avoid. Keep the language model for reading unstructured claims, which is the job it is uniquely good at.

06Where the model actually goes

It replaces the scoring formula. It does not replace the language model, and it does not move the human boundary.

Code Gathers evidence, applies hard rules — unchanged as today
Model Calibrated risk probability, replacing high=35 / medium=15 / low=5 new
LLM Reads unstructured claims, explains, covers the long tail — unchanged as today
Human Approves, rejects, escalates — unchanged as today

Keep the clamp. A model output that contradicts a hard rule is still a bug, and a learned score is not exempt from that check simply because it was learned. The override log stays the drift signal it already is.

07Stack, staged

Do not build the platform before the model. Each stage is only worth starting once the previous one has produced something.

08Evaluation

  • PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC. ROC flatters models badly at low base rates; with 2% positives a useless model still looks respectable.
  • Precision@k, where k is how many campaigns the team can actually review per day. That is the real operating metric, and it is the one to put in front of anyone deciding whether to fund the work.
  • Cost-sensitive thresholds. A false positive costs a legitimate organiser real money and costs the reviewer their trust in the tool. A false negative defrauds donors. These are not symmetric — choose the ratio deliberately rather than defaulting to 0.5.
  • Time-based splits, never random. A random split leaks the future into the past and inflates everything.

Slice by country and language — non-negotiable here

A model trained mostly on US and UK data will systematically over-flag campaigns from exactly the regions with no registry coverage, because absence of corroborating evidence correlates with missing public infrastructure rather than with fraud.

In aggregate that looks like accuracy. Sliced by country it is a fairness failure that falls hardest on the communities this platform exists to serve. Set per-slice minimums before training, not after the first incident.

09What to instrument now

Small schema changes today; the difference between having a usable dataset in a year and starting to collect one then.

[ ]Capture device fingerprint, IP and user agent on submission.
[ ]Capture payout destination — bank or wallet identifier, hashed — and flag when it changes.
[ ]Log whether the reviewer saw the AI recommendation before deciding.
[ ]Keep a small unassisted holdout where reviewers decide without seeing a recommendation.
[ ]Record time-to-decision per campaign; a proxy for difficulty and a target in its own right.
[ ]Join chargebacks, refunds and takedowns back to the campaign ID.
[ ]Version the feature extraction, so a row can be traced to the code that produced it.
[ ]Never mutate an assessment in place — append a new one.

The last two are the ones people skip and regret. Without a feature version you cannot tell whether a metric moved because the model changed or because the extraction did.
